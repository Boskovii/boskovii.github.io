Exoskeletons have the potential to transform human mobility, yet their benefits remain largely unrealized. A major challenge is providing assistance that reflects both the task a person is performing and what they prioritize in that moment, whether it be balance, speed, energy expenditure, comfort, or some combination of these objectives. I want to build exoskeletons that respond to these individual goals and that people **choose** to use in daily life.

I am currently a Research Fellow in Bioengineering at the [Harvard Biodesign Lab](https://biodesign.seas.harvard.edu/), where I am working with Prof. Conor Walsh on a powered ankle exoskeleton for people recovering from stroke. My PhD research interests center on three questions:

1. **How can we identify what an individual wants to gain from an exoskeleton and personalize the optimization objective accordingly?**
2. **How should assistance adapt as a person’s movement priorities and ability to use the device evolve?**
3. **How can we design exoskeletons that are practical for long-term use in homes and communities?**

At Georgia Tech, I designed hardware for a robotic hip exoskeleton and a human-in-the-loop plantarflexion dynamometer with Prof. Aaron Young in the [EPIC Lab](https://epic.gatech.edu/) and Prof. Gregory Sawicki in the [PoWeR Lab](https://power.me.gatech.edu/). I also wrote an undergraduate thesis proposing a method for evaluating physical human-robot interfaces across devices. Across these projects, I have gained hands-on experience prototyping and integrating wearable robotic hardware, conducting experiments with both unimpaired participants and people recovering from stroke, and collecting and analyzing biomechanical data. During my PhD, I hope to build on these skills while expanding my expertise in controls and embedded systems.
